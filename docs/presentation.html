01 The moment we designed for

08:50 on a Thursday. Twenty-four people due in ten minutes.

The manager is accountable for the floor. The data that predicts the floor sits in the transport system, in a format built for someone else.

What happens today

Assemble, then guess

  • Open the transport portal — a list of cabs and delay flags.
  • Cross-check the roster in a spreadsheet.
  • Ring three or four people who might be stuck.
  • Guess how short the queue will be.
  • Find out you were wrong at 09:10, from the call queue.

The signal is rich. The insight is missing. The actions are manual.

What the agent does

Answer, priced, with the message written

  • Watches every rostered agent's commute against the clock.
  • Projects the floor and the service level per queue.
  • Opens an alert when a threshold breaks — before nine, not after.
  • Names who can cover, from people verifiably already on the floor.
  • Hands back a message the manager can forward unedited.

Senses. Reasons. Acts. Without being asked.

The brief names three personas. We chose the one that nobody builds for — and the one where a twenty-minute delay turns into money in under an hour.

03 Who we built for

Three personas. We chose the one nobody builds for.

The first two operate the transport service. The third only lives with it — which is exactly why their tooling is missing.

Not this build

Transport manager

Owns vendor coordination, escalations, shift planning. Operates the service.

Well served by existing ops dashboards. We surface a vendor pattern to them, as one escalation option.

Not this build

Transport & facilities head

Owns budget, SLA accountability, leadership reporting. Strategic, monthly cadence.

Our alert output is already forwardable upward without rework — but their own view is out of scope.

Our persona

Team / line manager

Shift-based ops. Needs to know who made it, who was late, and how delays ripple into floor readiness.

  • Cannot call a vendor.
  • Cannot re-plan a route.
  • Can only move people on their own floor.
Why this constraint made the product better. Because the manager can only act on their own floor, every recommendation had to be executable within ten minutes by one person. No "contact the vendor" hand-waving. That is what forced us to compute cover candidates who are verifiably already at their desks.
04 How we thought about it

One row in the trip log rewrote the whole design

The disagreement

Transport measures the trip. The cab ran within tolerance, so the trip is clean and the operator's dashboard is green.

The manager lives with the floor. The desk was empty for twenty minutes and the call queue felt every second of it.

70% of arrivals that miss this shift's start are stamped NODELAY.

The design rule it produced

The product never reads delay_reason. Not once, anywhere in the codebase.

It derives lateness from the only thing the manager cares about: was a body in a chair when the shift bell rang.

The hard alert trigger is the deterministic "planned drop has passed, no arrival recorded" — which has no false positives, with or without GPS.

What the queue maths gives us

Erlang C service level, speed of answer, abandonment, occupancy, schedule adherence, a day-level rollup, and agents_needed — the last of which is what makes every option comparable on one number.

Honest about the model: Erlang C assumes Poisson arrivals, exponential handle times, no abandonment, and interchangeable agents. Real WFM tools relax all four. The shape of the answer is what the decision depends on.

12 Restraint as a feature

The agent also tells you when not to escalate

The interval
15%

Answered in 20 seconds, right now, on Billing Support. Against an 80% target. This looks like a five-alarm fire.

The day
92%

Sixteen intervals, zero breached, 736 calls. The day service level holds the 80% target. The contract is written on the day, not the interval.

“A twenty-minute gap does not break the day. The interval collapses; the daily number holds. The alert says so, and escalates to operations only when the day is genuinely at risk.”

Why this matters more than the alert itself

  • An alerting system that fires on every red interval gets muted within a week. Then it protects nothing.
  • Escalating a recoverable morning burns the manager's credibility with operations, and their own time.
  • Restraint is what makes the alert believable on the one morning it does say escalate now.

Not escalating saves as much money as the cover recommendation does.

13 The loop

Sense, reason, act — and the model is only in the third box

Sense

A clock over the trip feed

A replay clock advances one minute at a time over real trip events. Every read is guarded by now, so nothing downstream can see the future.

Swap the replay for a live event consumer and nothing else in the system changes. That is the whole production seam.

Reason

Deterministic Python, ~15 ms

Nine-state rider machine → arrival projection with an uncertainty band → queue headcount → Erlang C and the day rollup → cause → five benchmarks → cover candidates → hold-over cost → alert with priced options.

Zero model calls. Zero per-tick round trips.

Act

The agent writes and answers

A Google ADK agent receives the computed alert and writes the summary and the drafts. The same agent answers the manager's questions from the same computed board.

It cannot invent a number or a name, because the tools only hand it what was computed.

The trigger discipline

The model writes on five events, not on a timer

alert opens resolves cause changes recommendation changes severity band crossed

A 150-tick morning across two queues produces 11 narratives, not 300. Everything else is arithmetic. This one decision is most of the cost story on the next-but-one slide.

16 Tech stack

Chosen so the reasoning is the hard part

The brief allows any stack. We optimised for depth of reasoning per hour, and for a clean seam into an existing platform.

Data & store
  • PostgreSQL — trips, roster, alerts, actions, cover log, narrative cache, and agent sessions
  • SQL views carry the history maths, so benchmarks are a query not a job
  • COPY bulk load — 2.3M rows in ~90 s
  • psycopg 3 with a connection pool
  • pandas / pyarrow for the one-off load and quirk reconciliation
Reasoning & API
  • Python 3.12, pure functions, no framework in the core
  • Erlang C implemented directly — service level, ASA, abandonment, occupancy, adherence
  • FastAPI + uvicorn, 14 endpoints, one Scope dependency for tenancy
  • pytest — 112 offline tests, 3 live behind a marker
  • uv for dependency resolution and running
Agent & interface
  • Google ADK 2.8 — narrator agent (2 tools) and chat agent (7 tools)
  • LiteLLM adapter — so the model is a one-line swap: OpenAI, Gemini, or local ollama/llama3.2
  • ADK DatabaseSessionService on the same Postgres — one thing to back up
  • One HTML file, no build step, Inter + Poppins, MoveInSync palette
  • Playwright for reproducible screenshots
Why not Java and Angular? The brief calls them preferred, not required. In a fixed time box the scarce resource was reasoning depth, not language familiarity — and none of the four evaluation criteria are language-specific. The seam into their platform is an HTTP boundary and a Postgres schema, both of which are language-agnostic.
17 Agentic design

Where the model is, and where it is deliberately not

Computed in PythonWritten by the model
rider state, arrival, headcountwhich two of five facts to lead with
service level, day rollup, adherencethe five sentences of the summary
cause, triggers, option scoresthe message to the shift lead
who can cover, what it coststhe answer to a manager's question
The rule: if a number could end up in front of a director, a language model must not have produced it.

Why the numbers must be deterministic

  • They get forwarded to a director. They must be reproducible on demand.
  • Two options are compared on the same axis. If the axis wobbles, the comparison is meaningless.
  • They must be identical on a re-run, so a decision can be audited three weeks later.

Why the model earns its place anyway

  • Five benchmarks are available; leading with all five is unreadable at 08:55. Choosing two is judgement, and it is the model's job.
  • A message a manager will actually forward has to read like a person wrote it.
  • A follow-up question can take any shape. That is a natural-language problem.

The guarantee is structural, not a prompt. The tools return computed objects. There is no tool that returns raw rows, and scope is not settable by the model. It has nothing to hallucinate from.

18 Cost & latency at enterprise volume

Metered, not claimed — there is a /usage endpoint

Twenty percent of the rubric is inference cost, latency and efficiency at scale. So we measured it.

Cost of one tick
15 ms

And zero model calls. The expensive part of the product is free.

Narratives per morning
11

Across 150 ticks and two queues. A naive per-tick agent would make 300.

Tokens per narrative
~5k

Down from 22,600 in the first working version — a 4.5× cut.

Latency per narrative
~9 s

Detached from the clock, so the board never freezes waiting for a model.

How the cost was cut 4.5×

  • Facts moved out of the prompt and into tools. The model fetches what it needs instead of being handed everything.
  • A throwaway conversation per write-up. No transcript accumulating across a morning.
  • Narrative cache keyed by payload hash. The same situation next Tuesday costs nothing.
  • Five change triggers instead of a timer. This is the single biggest saving.

What this means at enterprise volume

The reasoning core does no model calls and no per-tick database round trips. Scaling to a thousand shifts scales a 15 ms pure function, which is an ordinary capacity problem with an ordinary answer.

Model spend scales with situations that changed, not with shifts, minutes, or users. A quiet morning at a well-run site costs nothing at all.

That is the difference between an agent you can deploy and a demo you can afford to run once.

Why MoveInSync should want to own this layer

Commute is procured on price and switchable. Floor readiness is not.

Move from a line item to a dependency

Transport is procured, benchmarked and re-tendered on price. The moment a client's line managers start their shift on this board, transport becomes operational infrastructure rather than a cost line.

Switching vendor now means retraining every floor manager, not re-running a price comparison.

BPO and 24/7 enterprise support

These contracts are won on service level and lost on hold-over and overtime. They are also the accounts where commute spend is highest and scrutiny is heaviest.

A late cab is their problem only once it becomes an unmanned desk. Nobody currently makes that translation. This does.

Zero new data collection

Pickup times, drop times, no-shows, vendors — MoveInSync already has all of it. This is a reasoning layer on data already being paid for and already flowing.

No new hardware, no new integrations, no client-side instrumentation. The margin on the second sale is very different from the first.

4 · A third seat at the client

Reach the line manager, not just the admin

Today MoveInSync's user is the transport admin. This product gives them a daily relationship with every line manager on every floor — dozens of users per site instead of one.

Daily-active line managers are the strongest renewal signal an enterprise product can have, and the natural route to a per-seat tier.

22 Deployability

Multi-tenancy, latency, and the gaps we chose

Production-shaped — keep
  • Tenant-scoped throughout. Every table and all 14 endpoints keyed by business_unit + office. Another tenant is two env vars.
  • ~15 ms per tick. No model on the clock, no per-tick DB round trip.
  • Durable in Postgres, including agent sessions. One thing to back up.
  • Graceful degradation. Model down → templates. Board never stops.
  • /health reports process, mode, and whether a model key is present.
Demo-only — do not ship as fact
  • The replay clock. A recorded morning, not a live consumer. This is the intended seam.
  • The night shift and handover. Asserted, because this site's log has no outbound legs near 09:00. Flagged synthetic on the roster row.
  • Queue AHT, call forecast, SLA target, overtime rate. Synthetic. Replaced by the client's WFM numbers.
  • The story slider. A presenter tool, not the product. It is app/timeline.py and it is clearly separated.
Deliberately not built
  • Auth, SSO, API keys. The brief explicitly does not expect them, and MoveInSync already has a signed-in desk.
  • Multi-region, sharding, an event bus. Their production plan already talks CDC and Kafka for trip events; that feed is the replacement clock.
  • GPS. Not in the dataset. See the limitations slide — we handle its absence rather than pretending.
  • The other two personas. Building one persona properly beats three shallowly, and the rubric rewards landing with the intended user.

Runs on Render, Vercel, or Cloud Run. The ADK agent deploys with one command.

23 Honesty

What is real, and what we invented

Every synthetic value is labelled in the code and in the roster, not only on this slide.

Real — from the dataset
  • Riders, trips, pickup and drop times, no-shows, vendors, cabs
  • The 24-person team — real riders, selected because they rode this shift on 40+ days
  • The 24-person cover pool — same, on the 08:30 shift
  • All five benchmarks: team norm over 64 days, weekday pattern over 12 Thursdays, recent trend, four peer sites, structural slack over 1,413 legs
  • The 46% late rate, the 70% NODELAY rate, the 5-vs-13-minute buffer finding
  • Every arrival time, every projection, every shortfall on the board
Synthetic — and flagged in code
  • Which queue a rider serves, handle time, call forecast, service target — in the roster and queues tables
  • Overtime rate, cab-home time, cover fairness counter — in app/config.py
  • The night shift and positional handover — asserted, and marked synthetic on the roster row

Why we invented the night shift, specifically. Clearwater's trip log has no outbound legs anywhere near 09:00, so there was no night shift in the data to read. We asserted one because positional handover — a night agent cannot leave until their relief is seated — is what makes a late arrival cost somebody their morning, rather than merely thinning a queue. Remove it and the product is a headcount widget.

The rule we held to: never let a synthetic number do work that a real one could do. The night shift is the single invention that changes the shape of the product, and it is the one we flag loudest.
24 Limitations

What we cannot do, and what we did about it

Limitation

No GPS in the dataset

Pickup and drop times only. Once a rider is aboard, nothing is observable until they arrive — ~13 minutes of irreducible spread.

What we did

Impact is reported as a range, never a false point estimate, and the ± spread shows on every arrival. The hard trigger is the deterministic "planned drop passed, no arrival" — no false positives, with or without GPS.

Limitation

The projection is optimistic on bad days

It is calibrated on ordinary mornings. On genuinely bad ones it under-predicts lateness.

What we did

This is asserted in a test, so nobody who inherits the code mistakes a known bias for a bug — and so it fails loudly if someone later "fixes" it in the wrong direction.

Limitation

Erlang C makes four assumptions

Poisson arrivals, exponential handle times, no abandonment, interchangeable agents. Real WFM tools relax all four.

What we did

Used it anyway, because the shape of the answer is what the decision depends on — 92% to 15% is the insight, not the third significant figure. It lives in one module, so a client's WFM engine can replace it wholesale.

Limitation

The night shift is invented

The most load-bearing synthetic element in the build. See the previous slide.

What we did

Labelled everywhere — config, roster row, README, and a slide before anyone asks. Hold-over minutes are not quoted as measured fact until a real logout roster exists.

The story slider is precomputed — and that is not a trick

Every minute from 07:30 to 10:00 is captured, with the landmarks derived from the event feed. Every snapshot is the same deterministic function the live clock runs, evaluated at that minute, with the same guard against reading the future. The narratives are real model output, produced in order, so the write-up at 08:25 is the one that was written at 08:25.

Why it exists

So that a slow network, a rate limit, or an empty API balance can never cost us a demo. "Back to live" returns to the running clock at any moment.
